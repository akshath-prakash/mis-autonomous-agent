# Executive Brief: Data Center Power Constraints in Northern California

**Audience:** Senior Leadership | **Basis:** Research summary drawn from background knowledge, not verified against primary sources. Items marked *(verify)* must be confirmed before any external use or commitment.

---

## Headline Takeaway

**Power availability is a primary gating factor for Bay Area data center growth.** Demand from AI and high-density loads has grown sharply since 2023. In constrained areas such as the San Jose/Santa Clara corridor, new large-load service is reported to take multiple years. We should plan for multi-year waits in the South Bay and diversify our siting strategy now.

---

## Key Findings

- **Demand is growing sharply.** Data center requests in PG&E territory have risen steadily since 2023. PG&E has publicly cited a pipeline of multiple gigawatts. Our recollection is roughly 5.5 GW in 2024 and roughly 10 GW in 2025 *(verify exact figures, dates, and the share assumed to energize by about 2030)*. It is unclear how much of the pipeline is speculative or duplicate requests.
- **Wait times are long.** Large-load connections in constrained Bay Area areas are reported as multi-year, because new substations and transmission upgrades are needed. A general industry range of about 4–7+ years applies where new substations or transmission are required. This is **not** a PG&E commitment *(verify)*. Specific project delay dates are unconfirmed.
- **Bottlenecks are physical.** They are South Bay substation capacity, transmission import limits into the Bay Area, and long lead times for large transformers and switchgear. Transformer lead times are often 2–4 years industry-wide.
- **The utility map matters.** Santa Clara is served by municipal Silicon Valley Power (SVP), not PG&E. SVP-served areas still depend on the CAISO-controlled transmission grid, much of which PG&E owns. Sacramento is served by SMUD. San Jose, the East Bay, and the Central Valley edges are PG&E territory.
- **Local supply is thin.** Gas plant retirements have tightened local capacity. The January 2025 Moss Landing battery fire removed a large regional storage resource *(verify capacity lost and return-to-service timeline)*.
- **Upgrades are planned, but details are unconfirmed.** PG&E's capital plan is very large, on the order of tens of billions of dollars per year across wildfire, undergrounding, reliability, and expansion *(verify total and share for load growth)*. CAISO's transmission plans approved several billion dollars of projects, including roughly $4.8B for 2024–25 *(verify which projects serve Bay Area load)*. Specific South Bay projects, in-service dates, and MW capacities are not confirmed.
- **Rules are in flux.** PG&E has proposed or pursued a CPUC Rule 30 process for large transmission-level loads, with cluster studies and financial commitments to filter speculative requests. The current status is unconfirmed *(verify)*. Outcomes of 2025 state data-center bills are also unconfirmed *(verify)*.
- **Affordability pressure is a factor.** California has some of the highest U.S. electricity rates. This shapes regulators' willingness to approve costly expansion for new large loads.

---

## Key Risks

1. **Schedule risk:** Regulatory and construction timelines may exceed our project timelines.
2. **Cost-allocation risk:** Who pays for grid upgrades is politically and regulatorily contested. Large loads may face higher financial commitments or tariff terms.
3. **Forecast risk:** Speculative or duplicate requests can distort planning. Over-building could strand costs, and under-building could delay credible projects.
4. **Supply chain risk:** Long equipment lead times affect both the utility and our on-site infrastructure.
5. **Sustainability and permitting risk:** Diesel backup generators require BAAQMD air permits. Behind-the-meter gas or diesel conflicts with state climate goals. Local land use, water, and cooling concerns also apply. No binding moratorium is known *(verify)*.
6. **Reliability and financial risk:** Storage and local generation gaps affect reliability. PG&E's wildfire liabilities and capital needs compete with load-growth investment.
7. **Regulatory timing risk:** CPUC and CAISO decisions may lag developer timelines.

---

## Recommended Actions

1. **Engage the utility early.** Submit credible, milestone-backed load requests and track Rule 30 outcomes.
2. **Diversify geography.** Evaluate SMUD territory, the Central Valley, and out-of-state sites. Central Valley sites are largely PG&E territory, so they do not avoid PG&E interconnection. Their capacity and timelines have not been verified in this research.
3. **Pursue bridging options.** Assess flexible or curtailable interconnection, on-site storage, and fuel cells. Their status in PG&E tariffs is unverified *(verify)*. Review air permitting and emissions up front.
4. **Secure long-lead equipment.** Consider pre-ordering transformers and switchgear for planned phases.
5. **Plan for costs.** Model higher interconnection costs and financial security requirements as a scenario.
6. **Verify before committing.** Confirm pipeline figures, project dates, Rule 30 status, and any new-service moratorium or allocation cap. Sources are PG&E earnings decks and filings, CPUC dockets, CAISO plans, SVP and City of Santa Clara materials, the BAAQMD permit database, and the California legislative record.

---

**Bottom line:** Treat power availability as a critical-path item in every Northern California expansion decision, and base timelines on confirmed utility schedules, not on assumed demand-driven speed.
